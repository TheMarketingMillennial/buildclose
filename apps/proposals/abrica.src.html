01The gap

A form collects answers.
It doesn't hold a case.

You take client intakes through Google Forms today, and there is no system for onboarding a client once the form has been submitted. The form captures the information. What happens next is arranged by hand.

That is not a criticism of how you work — it is what a form is. It collects, and then it forgets. A client relationship needs documents, a signature, a record of what was agreed, and somewhere for all of it to live while the matter is open.

i.

The hour after they submit

Someone filling in an injury enquiry is willing to act right now. By tomorrow they have spoken to another firm, or to a friend who told them to wait. The form has no way to hold that hour.

ii.

The documents you chase

Photographs, insurance cards, identification, the police report. A form cannot ask for them well, cannot accept them from a phone, and cannot file them against the right person without someone doing it by hand.

iii.

The record that isn't there

When a firm asks where a signed client came from, what they were told and when they agreed, a spreadsheet row is not an answer. That gap is also why it is hard to charge more for the work.

A form gives the firm a name in a spreadsheet. This gives the firm a workplace: a signed retainer, the documents attached, and a case that stays in one place until it closes.

If there is more to your current setup than this — a CRM, a case management system, something in between — tell me before we begin and I will adjust the scope around it. The description above is what I understand today, and I would rather correct it now than build on an assumption.

This is the middle column. It is not an attempt to replace Clio or Filevine — a firm that already runs one of those should keep it, and this should sit alongside it as the place a case lives from enquiry through acceptance and its first documents. Competing head-on with a full practice management system would mean building trust accounting, calendaring and time capture, and it would put the product in a price comparison it is not designed to win.

Why the middle column is the right one

A firm will not pay per case for a system that only collects enquiries — the value ends the moment the form is submitted. It will pay per case for a workspace that holds the matter while the file is being opened, because that is a period where the firm's expensive software is sitting empty and its staff are working out of an inbox. That is the gap this fills, and it is why per-case pricing is defensible rather than invented.

Not a generic form with a logo bolted on. The firm's name, its colours, its language. To the person filling it in, it is the firm's own intake — which is the point, because they are deciding whether to trust that firm.

The questions adapt to what happened

Someone struck by a truck is asked about the company that owned it. Someone who slipped in a store is asked who manages the property. Someone who has not seen a doctor is not asked which hospital they attended. The intake reads as attention, not as paperwork.

Injury photographs, insurance cards, identification, the police report — taken with a phone camera during the intake and filed by category. No second conversation about where to send things.

If the person says they already have a lawyer, the intake stops and no retainer is sent. It is the kind of error that becomes a real problem later, and it is removed rather than left to memory.

The firm's own retainer, executed electronically, with the certificate stored against the client. A signed retainer records the client's instruction; acceptance of the matter remains the firm's decision, as it must — but the instruction is on file.

05How it earns

A platform fee, and a fee per case the firm works.

You charge law firms two things: a monthly platform fee for the portal, and a monthly amount for each case being worked inside it. Nothing is charged for a case that is closed or archived.

Firm onboardingOne-timeBranding, retainer template, question set, testing, training

Two design decisions make this fair to the firm and clean commercially.

The firm can add cases that never touched the intake page. A client who walked into the office or telephoned is keyed in directly, and is billed at the same rate. That matters practically — most of a firm's cases arrive that way — and it means the fee is for the workspace the firm uses, not for the enquiry it received. Firms should be told this plainly, because it is the reason the model is defensible rather than extractive.

The firm sees its running count at all times. The billing section shows the number of billable cases, the projected invoice, and the history. Nobody should learn about a charge at the end of the month, and a case the firm believes was miscounted can be flagged for review from the same screen.

What this means for what you can charge

Per-case pricing means revenue grows with the firm's own case load rather than with the number of firms you sign — so a single busy practice can be worth more than three quiet ones. It also means the product has to earn its place every month. That is a harder commercial position than a flat subscription, and it is the right one: a firm that stops seeing value stops paying, which is a sharper feedback signal than a twelve-month contract.

08Scope

What is in Stage One — and what is deliberately not.

The exclusions matter as much as the inclusions. They are what keep this a fast, focused build rather than a practice management system that takes a year and competes with software the firm already owns.

Not included

Trust accounting · court calendar integration · legal time tracking · settlement management · advanced document automation · third-party case management integrations. These are what separate a case workspace from a full practice management system, and each is available in the software the firm already uses.

If any of the excluded items turns out to be essential to your first firm, tell me before week two. Adding one later is a change order rather than a surprise, and it is better priced before the architecture settles than after.

11What you own

Yours to operate, and yours to sell.

You own the client-specific components built for you, and you hold a perpetual, non-exclusive, worldwide licence to operate, brand, market and sell subscription access to the platform — including selling the business itself.

Every account the platform runs on is opened in your name: hosting, database, storage, domain, email, monitoring, e-signature. I hold administrative access while we work together and it is removed when the engagement ends. That is what makes the licence worth something — a perpetual right means little if the supplier holds the keys.

You also receive the source code, the infrastructure configuration, an architecture document and a runbook, so another qualified developer can pick the platform up and maintain it. Continuity is not a favour; it is what makes this an asset rather than a dependency.

Said plainly

The development cost does not create value on its own — revenue does. The build is what makes revenue possible; signing firms is the work that makes it worth anything. What you end up with is a product that has the potential to develop transferable enterprise value, not a guarantee of one.
